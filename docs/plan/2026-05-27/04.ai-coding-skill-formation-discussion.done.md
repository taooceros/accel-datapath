# Discussion presentation plan: The Illusion of Competence

## Goal

Prepare a 20--30 minute discussion-led presentation on Anthropic's "How AI assistance impacts the formation of coding skills" / "How AI Impacts Skill Formation."

This is not a paper-summary deck. It is a room argument:

> AI can help people finish code, but finishing code is not the same as building the skill needed to debug, review, and supervise code later.

The presentation should make classmates question the default productivity story around AI, then move the room toward concrete habits and tool-design choices.

Audience: human-AI interaction classmates who can follow basic programming examples but should not need prior knowledge of Trio.

Tone: provocative, conversational, and fair. The talk should challenge assumptions without turning into "AI bad."

## Sources

Primary:
- Judy Hanwen Shen and Alex Tamkin, "How AI Impacts Skill Formation," arXiv:2601.20245, 2026.
- Anthropic Research, "How AI assistance impacts the formation of coding skills," 2026.

Optional audience reference:
- Anthropic Research video on AI and skill formation, provided as a pre-watch / reference visual.

Claims that may appear on slides:
- The study recruited 52 mostly junior software engineers with Python experience and unfamiliarity with Trio.
- Participants learned Trio concepts through a warm-up, two coding tasks, and a final unassisted quiz.
- AI users averaged 50% on the quiz versus 67% for hand-coders, a 17 percentage-point gap described by the article as nearly two letter grades.
- AI users finished about two minutes faster on average, but the time difference was not statistically significant.
- The largest score gap was on debugging questions.
- Low-scoring AI-use patterns involved delegation, progressive reliance, or AI-led debugging; high-scoring patterns kept the learner engaged through comprehension, explanation, or conceptual inquiry.

Boundaries:
- Do not claim the paper proves permanent skill degradation; the measured outcome is immediate post-task mastery.
- Do not claim all AI use harms learning; the paper's own qualitative result is that interaction pattern matters.
- Do not overstate causality from the six qualitative interaction clusters.

## Design principle

The old academic-paper structure is the wrong center of gravity. The deck should be organized around the audience's decision problem:

> If AI tools are entering everyday work, what habits preserve the human expertise that future AI oversight depends on?

Use one recurring contrast:

```text
artifact path: ask AI -> get code -> task passes
learning path: predict -> try -> fail -> debug -> explain -> retain
```

Repeat this contrast in the hook, the result interpretation, the behavioral persona section, and the final discussion.

## Story spine

1. Start with the audience's lived behavior: people increasingly accept AI-generated code they cannot fully explain.
2. Name the hidden risk: a working artifact can create an illusion of competence.
3. Establish the study: a randomized test of learning an unfamiliar async library, followed by an unassisted evaluation.
4. Present the paradox: AI users did not get a statistically reliable speed win, but did show lower retained mastery.
5. Make it personal: the meaningful distinction is not AI versus no AI, but delegation versus learning-oriented interaction.
6. End by making the room choose: what should individuals, schools, teams, and tools do differently?

Closing message:

> The important design question is not "Should we use AI?" It is "When the goal includes future competence, how do we keep the human doing the thinking that creates competence?"

## Section timing

- Hook / tension: slides 1--3, about 3--5 minutes.
- Experiment: slides 4--5, about 3 minutes.
- Productivity paradox: slides 6--8, about 5 minutes.
- Behavioral personas: slides 9--11, about 7 minutes.

## Slide sequence

### Slide 1: "The Illusion of Competence"

Purpose: open with a memorable frame, not a literature-summary title.

Main takeaway: AI can make us look competent before we have actually built competence.

Slide-visible components:
- Title: "The Illusion of Competence"
- Subtitle: "AI assistance, coding skills, and the hidden cost of easy answers"
- Source line: "Shen & Tamkin, Anthropic Research, 2026"
- Two-path visual:
  - "artifact path: ask AI -> get code -> task passes"
  - "learning path: predict -> try -> fail -> debug -> explain -> retain"
- Small prompt: "Which path did you use the last time AI helped you code?"

Presenter note:
- Do not start by saying the paper proves AI is harmful.
- Start by asking whether a passing solution is evidence of human understanding.

### Slide 2: "The tension in white-collar work"

Purpose: connect the paper to everyday AI productivity hype.

Main takeaway: the short-term reward is visible; the long-term skill effect is hidden.

Slide-visible components:
- Left column: "What organizations see"
  - faster drafts
  - more completed tickets
  - fewer visible syntax errors
- Right column: "What may be missing"
  - weaker mental model
  - less debugging practice
  - less ability to reject plausible wrong code
- Discussion starter in a large quote:
  - "How many of us have approved or shipped code this week that we could not fully explain line by line?"

Presenter note:
- Let the question sit for a moment.
- The point is not guilt; the point is measurement. Teams measure shipped artifacts more easily than retained skill.

### Slide 3: "The supervision trap"

Purpose: explain why this is an HAI problem, not only a programming-education problem.

Main takeaway: if AI writes more code, human review becomes more important, not less.

Slide-visible components:
- Pipeline:
  - "AI generates" -> "human reviews" -> "system ships"
- Under "human reviews," list the needed abilities:
  - detect structural errors
  - read unfamiliar code
  - understand library semantics
  - say no to plausible output
- Closing line:
  - "Oversight is not a job title. It is a skill."

Presenter note:
- Emphasize safety-critical and infrastructure settings without making them the only use case.
- Transition: the paper asks whether AI-assisted learning builds or bypasses the skills needed for this review role.

### Slide 4: "The experiment in one picture"

Purpose: establish credibility quickly.

Main takeaway: the study isolates what participants retained after using or not using AI.

Slide-visible components:
- RCT timeline:
  - "52 Python developers"
  - "unfamiliar library: Trio"
  - "warm-up"
  - "two Trio coding tasks"
  - branch: "AI sidebar allowed" versus "no AI"
  - "final quiz: no AI"
- Callout:
  - "The quiz matters because it removed the assistant."

Presenter note:
- Mention that Trio is useful here because it is unfamiliar but realistic: a new async library someone might learn at work.
- Do not dwell on implementation details of Trio.

### Slide 5: "What counted as mastery?"

Purpose: prevent the audience from thinking this is just a syntax-memory quiz.

Main takeaway: the assessment targets skills needed to supervise AI-written code.

Slide-visible components:
- Three mastery cards:
  - "Debugging: identify why code fails"
  - "Code reading: understand what code does"
  - "Conceptual: know the library model"
- Small secondary note:
  - "Low-level code writing matters less when tools can retrieve syntax; supervision depends on reading, debugging, and concepts."
- Final evaluation label:
  - "unassisted"

Presenter note:
- Stress that debugging is central because AI-generated code can be confidently wrong.

### Slide 6: "Reality check: less mastery, not much speed"

Purpose: show the core quantitative surprise.

Main takeaway: the AI group got a significant learning penalty without a statistically reliable average speed gain.

Slide-visible components:
- Simple score comparison:
  - "AI group: 50%"
  - "No-AI group: 67%"
  - "gap: 17 percentage points"
- Time comparison:
  - "AI: about 2 minutes faster on average"
  - "not statistically significant"
- One-line interpretation:
  - "A finished task did not translate into retained mastery."

Presenter note:
- Say "percentage points" for precision, while acknowledging the article describes this as 17% lower / nearly two letter grades.
- Avoid implying every AI user performed poorly; the next section explains variation.

### Slide 7: "The vulnerability: debugging"

Purpose: connect the result to future oversight.

Main takeaway: the biggest gap was in the skill most needed when AI output is wrong.

Slide-visible components:
- Statement:
  - "Largest group gap: debugging questions"
- Three-step explanation:
  - "No-AI learners hit more errors"
  - "They had to repair those errors"
  - "That repair loop likely trained debugging judgment"
- Oversight implication:
  - "If AI removes the error path, it may also remove the practice path."

Presenter note:
- Mark "likely" clearly. The paper suggests this mechanism; it does not prove a causal mediation path.

### Slide 8: "Where did the time savings go?"

Purpose: challenge the assumption that generation automatically saves time.

Main takeaway: prompting, checking, and repairing model output can eat the apparent speed gain.

Slide-visible components:
- Compact list:
  - "some participants spent up to 11 minutes composing queries"
  - "some asked up to 15 questions"
  - "AI can produce code, but the workflow still includes steering, checking, and fixing"
- Quote-like takeaway:
  - "The model may be working; the learner may not be practicing."

Presenter note:
- This is where the talk pivots from "AI vs no AI" to "what kind of AI interaction?"

### Slide 9: "Behavioral personas: not all AI use is the same"

Purpose: make the data personal and discussion-ready.

Main takeaway: interaction pattern predicts the conversation we should have about AI use.

Slide-visible components:
- Quiet two-column table:
  - Column 1: "Offloading patterns, lower quiz scores"
    - "AI delegation: asks model to write the solution"
    - "Progressive reliance: starts engaged, ends by delegating"
    - "Iterative AI debugging: asks AI to diagnose and fix"
  - Column 2: "Learning-oriented patterns, higher quiz scores"
    - "Generation then comprehension: asks follow-up questions"
    - "Hybrid code + explanation: requests code with explanation"
    - "Conceptual inquiry: asks concepts, writes independently"
- Bottom line:
  - "The question is not whether AI appears in the workflow; it is who owns the hard thinking."

Presenter note:
- Avoid ranking the people morally. These are patterns under time pressure, and most workplaces reward completion.

### Slide 10: "Two tiny interaction traces"

Purpose: let the audience feel the difference between delegation and learning use.

Main takeaway: the same assistant can either bypass or support the practice loop.

Slide-visible components:
- Trace A: "Delegation"
  - "User: write the solution"
  - "AI: here is code"
  - "User: paste / pass"
  - "Later: cannot debug variant"
- Trace B: "Learning"
  - "User: what does this Trio concept mean?"
  - "User: predicts behavior"
  - "User: tries / hits error"
  - "AI: explains why"
  - "Later: can debug variant"
- Visual: artifact path versus learning path repeated.

Presenter note:
- Keep the traces tiny; they are not meant to be realistic transcripts.
- Use them to set up self-identification.

### Slide 11: "Which persona was your last AI session?"

Purpose: force self-assessment before moving to policy.

Main takeaway: people can change their AI habit only after naming the current habit.

Slide-visible components:
- Audience prompt:
  - "Think of the last time AI helped you code."
- Three diagnostic questions:
  - "Did I predict before asking?"
  - "Did I debug anything myself?"
  - "If the model disappeared, what could I still explain?"
- Pair-share instruction:
  - "Tell a neighbor: delegation, debugging-by-AI, or learning-oriented?"

Presenter note:
- This is a short interaction, not a long activity. Keep it to one or two minutes.

### Slide 12: "Strategic pivot: generator or coach?"

Purpose: move from individual behavior to tool and workflow design.

Main takeaway: AI tools can be designed to preserve effort instead of bypassing it.

Slide-visible components:
- Four design levers:
  - "Ask-before-answer: elicit a hypothesis first"
  - "Hint-before-fix: support debugging without taking it over"
  - "Explain-after-generation: require a comprehension checkpoint"
  - "Mode clarity: ship mode versus learn mode"
- Discussion question:
  - "Which lever would annoy you in production but help you while learning?"

Presenter note:
- Make the tradeoff explicit: friction is bad for throughput but may be good for training.

### Slide 13: "Discussion prompt 1: the junior developer dilemma"

Purpose: open the first heavy discussion.

Main takeaway: short-term delivery gains can conflict with the pipeline that creates senior judgment.

Slide-visible components:
- Prompt:
  - "If junior engineers use AI to close their delivery gap today, how do they build the foundation needed for senior decision-making tomorrow?"
- Follow-ups:
  - "Should AI use be scaffolded differently during onboarding?"
  - "What should a manager measure besides tickets closed?"
  - "Where should productive struggle be protected?"

Presenter note:
- Push for concrete practices, not abstract opinions.

### Slide 14: "Discussion prompt 2: scaffolded AI"

Purpose: focus debate on interface design.

Main takeaway: the product question is whether the assistant should optimize for answers or for human capability.

Slide-visible components:
- Prompt:
  - "Do we want an AI that gives completed code directly, or an AI that behaves like a peer reviewer / tutor?"
- Design choices to debate:
  - "answer-last"
  - "show tests first"
  - "Socratic debugging"
  - "require explanation before merge"
  - "learning mode by default for unfamiliar APIs"

Presenter note:
- Invite disagreement. In real systems, sometimes answer-first is correct.

### Slide 15: "Discussion prompt 3: safety-critical risk"

Purpose: connect the paper to high-stakes environments.

Main takeaway: small uncertain speed gains may not justify weaker structural understanding where failure matters.

Slide-visible components:
- Prompt:
  - "In security, infrastructure, finance, or healthcare, is a minor unverified speed gain worth a drop in structural understanding?"
- Follow-ups:
  - "What tasks should require human explanation?"
  - "Should teams audit AI reliance the same way they audit tests?"
  - "What evidence would convince you the tool preserves skill?"
- Closing decision:
  - "What one habit should our team / class adopt on Monday?"

Presenter note:
- Do not overclaim the 17-point gap as permanent. Use it as a warning signal that skill should be measured, not assumed.

### Slide 16: "Bibliography"

Purpose: satisfy course requirements and leave sources visible.

Main takeaway: the discussion is grounded in the primary paper and article.

Slide-visible components:
- Shen, Judy Hanwen, and Alex Tamkin. "How AI Impacts Skill Formation." arXiv:2601.20245, 2026.
- Anthropic Research. "How AI assistance impacts the formation of coding skills." 2026.
- Anthropic Research video on AI and skill formation. YouTube reference link.
- Optional context citations if used in the spoken framing:
  - Anthropic productivity-gains work referenced by the article.
  - Prior coding-productivity studies referenced by the article.
  - Critical-thinking / cognitive-offloading work referenced by the article.

Presenter note:
- Keep this slide simple. Do not spend presentation time reading it.

## Visual guidance

Use a plain, discussion-first visual style:
- left-aligned text
- one calm accent color
- sparse rules or separators
- no decorative screenshots
- one quiet table for the six interaction patterns
- repeated artifact-path versus learning-path motif

Avoid:
- dense academic-method slides
- too many cards
- charts that look more precise than the article supports
- burying discussion prompts after a long literature review

## Aesthetic revision

The drafted deck should move away from the box-heavy project-report look and toward an editorial seminar aesthetic:
- warm paper background instead of stark white
- deep navy text with muted coral / amber / green accents
- large typographic hierarchy and generous whitespace
- fewer bordered cards; use thin rules, soft panels, and open layouts
- recurring visual motif of two paths: "artifact path" versus "learning path"
- section divider slides for the five-part structure
- result slides should feel like visual arguments, not dashboard widgets
- discussion slides should look like prompts for a room, with one large question and a few small anchors

The design should still preserve all slide-visible claims and caveats from the plan, but it should feel like a polished course discussion deck rather than an internal status report.

## Presenter-note guidance

Notes should carry the nuance. Slides should carry the argument.

Include notes for:
- exact caveats around immediate quiz versus long-term skill
- the distinction between percentage-point gap and "17% lower" article phrasing
- reminding the speaker not to moralize individual AI users
- transitions into audience discussion

## Reader-test checklist

A fresh reader should be able to answer:
- What is the provocative question of the talk?
- What did the study actually test?
- What result challenges the productivity story?
- Why is debugging the key oversight skill?
- What are the six interaction patterns?
- What concrete discussion should the class have after the summary?

If a slide does not support one of those answers or one of the three discussion prompts, cut it.
